- **Cross-sportsbook middling and arbitrage ALREADY EXIST**, scoped to spread markets only, across the 5-6 traditional-sportsbook feeds in `server/services/book-feeds.js` (oddstrader/sbrscrape, Pinnacle, Kambi/BetRivers, Bovada, FanDuel):
  - `server/services/nfl-shopping-board.js:229` `priceMiddle({homeLine, awayLine, homePrice, awayPrice})` — prices a middle exactly against the empirical signed-margin PMF (`signedMarginDistribution()`, line 46), correctly handles pushes, and flags a true arbitrage via `arbitrage: impliedProb(homePrice) + impliedProb(awayPrice) < 1` (line 259).
  - `server/services/nfl-shopping-board.js:267` `findMiddles({limit})` — scans `simultaneousQuotes('spreads')` (hard-coded to the spreads market only, line 268), requires `home.book !== away.book` (line 284), and reports `arbitrage_found` in `executionBoardSummary()` (line 422).
  - This is real, working code with the right math (empirical PMF, not assumed-normal; correct push handling). It has never been extended past spreads, and it has never been joined to a prediction-market leg.
- **Kalshi and Polymarket ingestion exist as a completely separate, parallel universe** that never touches the above:
  - `server/services/prediction-markets.js` — `captureKalshi()`, `captureKalshiFlow()`, `exchangeVsBook()` (line 173: compares Kalshi's moneyline price against a book spread converted through an ASSUMED normal distribution, `SIGMA=14.16`, not an actual no-vig book moneyline), `venueCostComparison()` (Kalshi fee model, explicitly notes Kalshi is moneyline-only).
  - `server/services/polymarket.js` — `ingestPolymarketNfl()`, `captureOrderBooks()` (captures real bid/ask/bid_size/ask_size but nothing downstream consumes size for fill simulation), `polymarketCost()`.
  - Neither module calls `priceMiddle()`/`findMiddles()`, and neither is called by them. `exchangeVsBook()` computes a probability *gap*, never a stake split, hit probability, or EV the way `priceMiddle()` does for two sportsbooks.
- **What is genuinely missing** (confirmed absent by grep across `server/`): any combinatorial cross-market-type arbitrage/middle logic (e.g., Kalshi moneyline vs. sportsbook spread, the mechanism the UCLA Polymarket paper below measures), any fill/liquidity-aware execution simulator, and any treatment of Kalshi's maker/taker asymmetric cost beyond the flat mechanical fee.

This reframes the deliverable precisely: the new capability is **cross-venue (prediction-market ↔ sportsbook) and cross-market-type (moneyline ↔ spread) arbitrage/middle detection**, generalizing code that already exists and works for the same-type/same-venue case.

## 1. Primary sources (read in full unless noted)

### S1. Franck, E., Verbeek, E., & Nüesch, S. (2013). "Inter-market Arbitrage in Betting." *Economica*, 80(318), 300–325. https://doi.org/10.1111/ecca.12009 — **read in full**
- **Sample:** 11,933 European soccer matches, top 5 leagues (EPL, Ligue 1, Bundesliga, Serie A, Primera División), 7 seasons (2004/05–2010/11). Odds from 10 bookmakers (B365, Blue Square, Bwin, Gamebookers, Interwetten, Ladbrokes, Sportingbet, Stan James, VC Bet, William Hill) matched against contemporaneous Betfair exchange odds.
- **Method:** Derives closed-form hedged-bet returns for (a) intra-market arbitrage (best odds across bookmakers alone) and (b) inter-market arbitrage (bookmaker vs. exchange, both a "long" resale strategy and a "short" direct-sell-at-the-exchange strategy), accounting for bookmaker overround and the exchange's per-trade commission (2–5%, vs. bookmaker overround averaging 11.3%).
- **Result:** Only 102 intra-market (bookmaker-vs-bookmaker) opportunities in the full sample. **2,287 inter-market (bookmaker-vs-exchange) opportunities — arbitrage available in more than 1 in 6 matches — with an average return of 1.4%.** The gap is driven by inefficiently *low-priced* bookmaker odds that can be resold higher on the exchange; bookmakers running these positions lose money on the bookmaker leg (-11.3% avg) and lose slightly on the hedge (-0.2%) — i.e., the arbitrage is real money moving from books to exchange arbitrageurs. Authors argue bookmakers deliberately post some unfavorable prices as a customer-acquisition cost, which the exchange's zero-switching-cost anonymity does not replicate — a structural, not accidental, feature.
- **Limitation stated:** Betfair's dataset is cross-sectional, not longitudinal, at the trade level, so quote timing is approximated; results are for one product family (3-way soccer moneyline) and one exchange, from a period (2004–2011) well before automated market-making was ubiquitous on that exchange.
- **Relevance to Gridiron:** This is the closest historical precedent to "sportsbook vs. exchange arbitrage," and the mechanism (bookmaker posts an inefficiently generous price; exchange resells it) is structurally the same shape as a Kalshi/Polymarket-vs-sportsbook play. The **1.4% average return, in a market with humans setting bookmaker prices and a comparatively illiquid, semi-manual exchange**, is the historical upper bound for how good inter-market arbitrage gets. It is directly contradicted in scale by S2 below, which measures the same mechanism in a fully electronic, blockchain-settled venue and finds it has been arbitraged down to near-zero.

### S2. Cheng, G., Yang, J., & Zou, H. (2026). "Arbitrage Analysis in Polymarket NBA Markets." UCLA, arXiv:2605.00864. — **read in full**
- **Sample:** 173 NBA games, Feb 4 – Mar 4, 2026. 75,088,497 limit-order-book (Level 1) snapshots polled every 3.6–5.5 seconds via Polymarket's CLOB API, across 3,042 single markets and 8.59 million reconstructed combinatorial states (Moneyline × Spread pairs).
- **Method:** Two detection pathways, each requiring a minimum $10 USDC executable-liquidity floor and strict deduplication against Polymarket's mirrored-liquidity book design: (1) single-market — complementary Yes/No prices deviating from summing to $1.00; (2) combinatorial — the mathematical fact that {Spread cover} ⊂ {Moneyline win}, so `Bid(Spread) > Ask(Moneyline)` is a risk-free mispricing, executed via a synthetic short (buying the complementary spread token) since Polymarket disallows direct shorting.
- **Result:** **Single-market: only 7 executable in-game episodes across the entire month**, median persistence 3.6 seconds, capped profit (at $100/trade) **$210.19 total** across all 173 games. **Combinatorial: 290 episodes** (median 2/game), concentrated in the final minutes, median yield 101 bps, capped profit **$559.59 total**; 76.9% of episodes bottlenecked to ~14.8 shares of executable size by shallow order-book depth. The theoretical "Middle" double-payout jackpot (both the Moneyline and Spread contract paying $1 because the final margin lands exactly in the gap) was **defined mathematically but never once realized empirically across all 290 episodes** — it is a real payoff structure that the data show essentially never triggers in practice.
- **Limitation stated (by the authors):** episodes resolving within a single 3.6–5.5s polling interval evade detection entirely, so reported frequency is a conservative lower bound; the sample is one month of one sport (NBA) and generalization to other sports/contract types is explicitly flagged as uncertain.
- **Relevance to Gridiron:** This is the single most important number in this research packet. It says: in the venue Gridiron already ingests (Polymarket), for the closest sport-analog to NFL that has enough volume to study, true single-market arbitrage is **microstructurally almost extinct** ($210 total, month-long, whole-league) and even the more frequent combinatorial version caps out at **~$560 total** with most of it unexecutable at more than token size. NFL has roughly 1/8th the games-per-week of the NBA and, per Gridiron's own `polymarket.js` comments, thinner depth outside futures markets — so the realistic expectation for NFL is *lower*, not higher, than these NBA numbers.

### S3. Bürgi, C., Deng, W., & Whelan, K. (2026). "Makers and Takers: The Economics of the Kalshi Prediction Market." University College Dublin working paper. https://www.karlwhelan.com/Papers/Kalshi.pdf — **read in full** (introduction, literature review, market-mechanics sections; headline results confirmed directly from body text)
- **Sample:** Transaction-level data on 46,282 distinct Kalshi contracts across 12,403 events, 2021 through April 2025 (Kalshi's sports contracts only launched "early 2025," so this sample is overwhelmingly non-sports — politics, culture, weather, finance — with sports a small, recent tail). Over 300,000 individual prices once both traded sides are counted.
- **Method:** First systematic study of Kalshi pricing. Directly identifies which side of every trade was Maker vs. Taker (Kalshi records this explicitly, avoiding noisy trade-classification algorithms used in equity microstructure). Builds a theoretical model (heterogeneous beliefs, self-selection into Maker/Taker roles under Kalshi's pre-2025 fee structure that charged Takers only) to explain the empirical pattern.
- **Result:** Kalshi prices are informative and become more accurate approaching close, but show a **clear, statistically significant favorite–longshot bias**: contracts priced under 10¢ lose investors **over 60% of their money** on average; contracts above 50¢ earn a small positive return. **Overall average return across all Kalshi contracts is about −20%.** Both Makers and Takers show the bias, but it is markedly worse for Takers — i.e., the side that *accepts* a posted quote (which is what any arbitrage/middle detector would do when it "buys" a Kalshi leg) is the systematically-disadvantaged side.
- **Limitation stated:** the sample predates most of Kalshi's sports-contract volume, so these numbers are a general Kalshi-microstructure finding, not an NFL-specific one; the paper's own theoretical model is calibrated, not independently validated out-of-sample.
- **Relevance to Gridiron:** `server/services/polymarket.js`'s `kalshiFee()` (line 292) already models Kalshi's mechanical trading fee correctly, but nothing in the codebase models this *adverse-selection* cost. Any arb/middle detector that treats a captured Kalshi quote as a clean, tradeable price — the way `exchangeVsBook()` does today — will systematically overstate the Kalshi leg's true expected value, especially on the low-probability side of a spread-derived moneyline (exactly where Gridiron's dog-shrinkage problem, per tonight's FOUND findings, already lives).

### S4. Ramesh, S., Mostofa, R., Bornstein, M., & Dobelman, J. (2019). "Beating the House: Identifying Inefficiencies in Sports Betting Markets." arXiv:1910.08858 (Federal Reserve Board / Rice University / U. Maryland). — **read in full**
- **Sample:** Historical point spreads, moneylines, and results from 16 Las Vegas sportsbooks, 1990–2017, for NFL, NBA, NCAAF (Power Five only), NCAAB, WNBA (excluding preseason, including playoffs). Line data scraped from vegasinsider.com and, for older years, archive.org snapshots.
- **Method:** Non-parametric win-probability model `P(Win | PS) = historical win% of teams at that exact posted spread` (avoids the small-spread-only limitation of the standard Stern normal-approximation model), applied per-book to find positive-expected-value bets created by *dispersion across the 16 books quoting the same game* — i.e., cross-sportsbook mispricing, not cross-venue exchange arbitrage. Validated with a 10,000-iteration bootstrap per sport, with Bonferroni-corrected 99% one-sided confidence intervals to guard against the multiple-comparisons problem the paper explicitly tests for (5 sports at once).
- **Result:** Randomly betting spreads market-wide returns about **−4.4%** (the baseline vig). Their EV-threshold + "epsilon" (favorite-longshot-tail-exclusion) betting rule produces a **statistically significant positive optimal ROI in every one of the 5 sports** under the bootstrap, with confidence intervals excluding zero after multiplicity correction.
- **Limitation stated:** the paper itself flags reliance on independent $1 bets (no compounding/Kelly), and — not stated by the authors but visible on inspection — the optimal Epsilon/EV-threshold hyperparameters are *fit to the same historical sample used to report the ROI*, which is exactly the "21-model historical search with no preregistration" failure mode tonight's FOUND findings already flag elsewhere in Gridiron; the bootstrap shows the fitted parameters are non-zero with confidence, not that they would have been chosen the same way out-of-sample.
- **Relevance to Gridiron:** confirms that price *dispersion across many books quoting the same game* is a real, historically-measured source of edge distinct from any forecasting skill — which is the same mechanism `nfl-shopping-board.js`'s `bestExecution()`/`findMiddles()` already exploits for spreads. It does **not** provide evidence for prediction-market-vs-sportsbook arbitrage specifically; it is cited here as the closest rigorous analog for the "shop many venues, no forecast needed" half of the topic, with an honest caveat about its own overfitting risk.

### S5. Marshall, B. (2009). "How quickly is temporary market inefficiency removed?" *Quarterly Review of Economics and Finance*, 49(3), 917–930. — secondary (abstract/summary only, not fetched in full; paywalled)
- **Key result (via search-engine summary of the published abstract):** studies duration of internet sports-betting arbitrage opportunities; median duration ~15 minutes, longest just under a day; arbitrageurs correct many opportunities within minutes and most within an hour; harder-to-find opportunities persist longer.
- **Relevance:** complements S2's finding of sub-4-second persistence in a fully automated blockchain order book — shows the decay-of-persistence trend (manual bookmaker era: minutes; automated exchange era: seconds) that any Gridiron system would be fighting.

### S6. Simon, J. R. (2024). "Inefficient Forecasts at the Sportsbook: An Analysis of Real-Time Betting Line Movement." *Management Science*, 70(12), 8583–8611. — secondary (abstract/summary only; full text paywalled, direct fetch attempts blocked)
- **Key result (via search-engine summary):** 3,681 MLB games, opening-to-close line movement across 4 sportsbooks. Finds simple strategies exploiting line movement (the mechanism underlying middling) would have yielded significant profit; forecasts do not improve monotonically as game time approaches; changes are significantly negatively autocorrelated (lines overreact then partially revert) — direct evidence that middling-style exploitation of overreaction is a real, published, peer-reviewed finding, not just a bettor folk theory.
- **Relevance:** grounds the general middling mechanism (line overreacts, moves back) independent of venue; Gridiron's own `nfl-shopping-board.js` middle-finder already operationalizes the same-book-network version of this.

### S7. Saguillo, O., Ghafouri, V., Kiffer, L., & Suarez-Tangil, G. (2025). "Unravelling the Probabilistic Forest: Arbitrage in Prediction Markets." IMDEA Networks / Oxford Internet Institute, arXiv:2508.03474 (AFT 2025). — secondary (abstract/summary only)
- **Key result (via search-engine summary):** across all of Polymarket (not sports-specific), ~$40M in realized arbitrage profit extracted over a ~1-year window across 86M+ trades and 7,000+ markets, split between intra-market ("Market Rebalancing") and inter-market ("Combinatorial") arbitrage.
- **Relevance:** the $40M figure sounds large but is platform-wide, mostly political/crypto/culture markets over a full year — S2's month-long, sports-only, $560-total finding is the number that actually bears on NFL, and the two together show sports is a tiny, liquidity-starved slice of Polymarket's total arbitrage activity.

## 4. Honest bottom line

Every dollar figure in the best primary source that actually measures this mechanism in a modern, liquid, sports-specific electronic venue (S2) is retail-toy-sized: **$210 in single-market arbitrage and $560 in combinatorial arbitrage, total, across an entire NBA month.** NFL should be expected to do worse, not better, given roughly 1/8th the game volume and Gridiron's own documented observation that Polymarket NFL depth outside futures is thin. The honest framing for Nick is: this is worth building as **measurement infrastructure and a genuinely new signal source** (in the same spirit `prediction-markets.js` already states for its own whale-flow work — "nothing here claims an edge yet"), not as a revenue plan. The Franck et al. (2013) 1.4%-average, 1-in-6-matches number is the *best* historical case for this mechanism, and it comes from a slower, more manual, pre-automated-market-making era that no longer describes Kalshi or Polymarket today.
