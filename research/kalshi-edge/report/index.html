Two of six passed. Neither was a money test.

Six claims were fixed in the repository before any result from 1 October onward was read. They were then run once on those games. The correction for running six tests at once is Holm's.

Why the holdout mattered

The rule that looked best failed. A plainer one did not.

Both rules sell the heavy underdog to people buying it as a lottery ticket. The first re-posts its offer every minute once the game is under way. The second places one order before kickoff and leaves it. Fills are the pessimistic kind: the order only counts as filled when the market trades straight through it.

The measurement that held

Order size tells you who is trading.

The public tape carries no names. It does carry sizes. Apps let people buy in dollars, so a "Buy $10" order at 50c prints as 19.32 contracts, which cost $9.9981 with the fee. An order that lands on a whole dollar like that is a retail dollar-buy. An order for a round number of contracts is something else.

It shows up in the next minute's price too, more weakly than I first said

For every in-game minute, take each group's net buying as a share of that minute's dollars, then see where the quote midpoint is a minute later. Integer orders are followed by the price moving their way. Retail dollar-buys are followed by about a third as much, or nothing.

Where the money goes

Takers lose about of what they stake. Most of it is the fee.

Every fill has a taker and a maker, and what the taker loses before fees is exactly what the maker gains. Adding up every settled sports fill gives the whole split.

The lead, and tonight's paper orders

One order before kickoff.

In a game-winner market, when the underdog's YES is offered between 3c and 10c ten minutes before the start, join that offer and leave it until 45 minutes in. Selling YES at 6c is the same position as buying NO at 94c. It wins about 95 times in 100 and loses the whole stake the other five.

What I would do, in order

  1. Grade the paper ledger for two weeks. The scanner writes tickets; grade settles them. The backtest filled about 13 positions a day in October and November, so two weeks is roughly 180. Until then the lead is a backtest.
  2. Find out what the queue does to you. Place twenty of these by hand at one contract each, about $19 at risk in total. The only thing the tape cannot show is whether a real order at the back of the line gets filled before the price leaves.
  3. Leave the in-game version alone. It lost 1.6% on the holdout, worst in esports and table tennis, where a game is nearly over within the rule's 45-minute window.
  4. Do not pay the taker fee to chase any of this. Every marketable-order rule tested came out at zero or worse after fees.
